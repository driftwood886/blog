Eugene Wigner's 1960 puzzle has always struck me as philosophically beautiful in a slightly irritating way. He observed that pure mathematics — developed for aesthetic or intellectual reasons, often with no physical application in mind — keeps turning out to describe the physical world with uncanny precision. Matrix algebra, invented by Cayley and Sylvester decades earlier, turned out to be exactly what quantum mechanics needed. Tensor calculus, developed by Riemann and Ricci-Curbastro for abstract geometry, was precisely the language general relativity required. Wigner called this "unreasonable" and was honest enough to say he had no explanation for it.

The standard reply from structural realists is: *of course* mathematics fits physics — because physics describes the relational structure of the world, and mathematics is the study of structure in general. On ontic structural realism (Ladyman, French), physical objects don't have intrinsic natures that exist independently of their relations. An electron isn't a "thing" that also happens to have charge and spin; it *just is* a bundle of structural relations — a position in a pattern. On this view, Wigner's mystery dissolves. The world is structure; mathematics studies structure; naturally they match.

I find this satisfying for about thirty seconds, and then I notice it's actually pushed the mystery back rather than resolved it.

If the physical world is a mathematical structure, then our universe is *one* mathematical structure among the infinitely many that mathematics describes. Why this one? Why do the specific structures corresponding to SU(3) × SU(2) × U(1) gauge symmetries, with these specific coupling constants, show up in the world we inhabit, while the (mathematically equally valid) structures corresponding to, say, different gauge groups or coupling constants don't? The structural realist has traded one puzzle — why does math fit the world? — for another: why does this particular piece of mathematics fit the world?

Max Tegmark bites the bullet here: *every* mathematical structure exists, and we find ourselves in one of them by anthropic reasoning. This is coherent, but it's basically swapping a philosophical puzzle for an infinite multiverse. Structural realism, in its more modest forms, doesn't go there — but then it owes us an explanation of what makes one structure *physical* that isn't just re-importing the notion of "physical" it was supposed to explain.

---

This crystallized for me when I was thinking about the Deng-Hani-Ma result from last year: a claimed proof of Hilbert's Sixth Problem, deriving the Boltzmann equation from Newtonian mechanics in the Boltzmann-Grad limit. It's a remarkable piece of mathematics. But I keep snagging on something.

The Boltzmann-Grad limit is defined by taking N → ∞ particles while simultaneously shrinking their diameters so that the mean free path stays fixed. In this limit, the density of the gas goes to zero. Real gases are not dilute. Dense fluids — which is most of the regime where Navier-Stokes equations and fluid dynamics actually apply — are outside the limit's scope.

So what exactly has been "derived"?

What's been established is a precise mathematical relationship: the statistical behavior of the hard-sphere system *converges* to Boltzmann equation solutions *in that particular limit*. This is a structural fact about two mathematical theories: they are, in the limit, structurally related in a specific way.

But a derivation in physics presumably means something stronger than this. It means the higher-level theory (Boltzmann) describes what the lower-level theory (Newton) actually produces, in physical conditions that actually obtain. The mathematical derivation establishes a *formal* connection; whether that formal connection licenses a *physical* grounding depends on whether the limit is physically realized or physically meaningful as an approximation.

The word "derives" is doing two entirely different kinds of work, and we let it slide.

---

This matters because we often talk as if mathematical derivations between theories settle physical questions about reduction and emergence. If we can mathematically derive thermodynamics from statistical mechanics, we tend to say thermodynamics is *nothing but* statistical mechanics at the macro scale. If we can derive Boltzmann from Newton (in some limit), we say irreversibility is *nothing but* Newtonian mechanics in disguise — and the arrow of time is "explained."

But mathematical derivations are relationships between structures. They hold in the abstract. The physical significance of that relationship depends entirely on whether the limit, the idealization, the approximation, accurately captures the physical situation. And that's a separate question — one that the mathematics itself can never answer.

Structural realism is right that physics describes structure. It's right that the persistence of structure across theory change (Fresnel to Maxwell, classical to quantum mechanics) is the best explanation for the success of science. But in celebrating the structural content of physics, it risks blurring a distinction that matters: the difference between *structural relationships between theories* and *physical reduction of one domain to another*.

Derivation in mathematics: we establish a formal relationship between two structures, valid in some limit or under some conditions.

Derivation in physics: we explain why high-level phenomena are the way they are, by showing that the underlying physical reality produces them under actual physical conditions.

These can come apart. And when they do — when the mathematical derivation requires idealizations that aren't physically realized — we should be careful about what we're claiming to have explained.

Wigner's puzzle doesn't dissolve. It reappears as: why does the structural correspondence between mathematical theories track the physically relevant relationships between physical systems? That's still unreasonably surprising, and we should be honest about not knowing the answer.
