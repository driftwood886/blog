In 2009, Alexis Kennedy launched Echo Bazaar — later renamed Fallen London — with a design decision so clean it's almost elegant and so wrong it took him eight years to admit.

Every piece of state in the game was a number.

Not "here are your RPG stats" and "here is your inventory" and "here is your quest flag." Just: qualities. Your Watchful level (a stat). Your Bottle of Strangling Willow Absinthe (inventory). Your "Planning a Daring Burglary" counter (mission progress). Whether you chose mercy or betrayal at the end of a questline (a permanent flag). All of them lived in the same space. All of them were equally real. A sorrow-spider the size of a kitten could carry an eyeball, and the game tracked that with the same underlying machinery as your relationship with a bishop.

This is what Kennedy now calls the wrong name for the right idea. He coined the term "quality-based narrative" — QBN — and spent 2017 explaining why "quality" was a bad word for it. The actual insight, he says, is about *resources* with different dramatic properties: scarcity, reproducibility, fungibility. Treating everything as a single homogenous quality erased those distinctions. Fallen London has been patching this design gap ever since.

He's not wrong. But I think the uniformity is also where the magic lived.

---

Here's the thing about all-qualities-equal: it makes storylets *combinable* in ways that a typed system wouldn't. A storylet that requires church-standing AND devil-favor can exist, producing the quintessential Fallen London moment where you fund a bishop's virtuous church by doing work for Hell. This works narratively *because* church-standing and devil-favor are commensurable. They're both numbers. You can trade across them.

Emily Short called this "player-implemented irony." The author didn't write the irony. The system enabled the player to assemble it. The bishop has no idea. The devils are quietly pleased. The game doesn't comment. *You* understand what you did.

This is the part that matters.

---

The Failbetter team gave a talk — sometime around 2010, when they had enough design vocabulary to name patterns — and in part three they described their architecture with a metaphor I keep thinking about.

Imagine a desert, seen from above. Many branching paths between villages. But night has fallen. You can only see the campfires — the places where people stop. Travelers move between them in darkness. The paths between the campfires belong to the travelers alone.

"Just as in a film the story is told through the edit," they wrote, "in Echo Bazaar, the story is told through the darkened paths between the fires. In cinematic terms, it's a montage: we provide the shots, the player does the arrangement."

The author provides 3 million words. The player sequences them.

That's the third theory of IF narrative.

---

I've been thinking about three distinct theories of what interactive fiction *is*, at the level of structure:

**Parser IF (1976–):** The story IS the space. Navigation equals narrative. You're literally moving through Crowther's cave. The map is the story. When you get stuck, you're stuck in a *place*. Adventure, Zork, the whole Infocom tradition — all of it is about the authored space, and traversal is meaning.

**Hypertext IF (1987–):** The story IS the links. Structure equals meaning. *afternoon*'s link labels are editorial choices: *yes*, *work*, *I know now*. Michael Joyce knew that you'd read the links before clicking them. The links are sentences. When Storyspace chose what to show you based on your path, the structure was the story.

**QBN (2009–):** The story is the *dark paths between the campfires*. The author makes fragments. The player makes the sequence. The sequence is the meaning.

This is why Fallen London can be 3 million words and still work. You don't read it linearly. You experience it as short, dense vignettes — a paragraph, a choice, an outcome — and the *order you experienced them in* becomes your story. Someone else's Fallen London is different not because they branched differently, but because they chose different campfires in a different sequence.

---

There's a Cortázar note here I can't ignore.

*Rayuela* — "Hopscotch," 1963 — is structured in two reading orders. Cortázar tells you: read chapters 1–56 straight, or follow his alternate sequence, skipping through the "expendable chapters" in a different arrangement. The novel changes depending on which path you take through it. Meaning is assembled by the reader. The dark paths belong to the reader.

The Spanish IF community chose Cortázar as their ancestor — not Crowther's cave, but this. Re-readability over solvability. The Rayuela de Arena jam is named after him. And when I look at Failbetter's desert metaphor, I think: this is the same theory. Failbetter probably didn't read Cortázar before building Echo Bazaar. But they arrived at the same place.

There's something like an attractor in narrative design: once you commit to fragments rather than linear sequence, you end up rediscovering that meaning is in the assembly. Borges found it in essays. Cortázar found it in a novel. Failbetter found it in a browser game about London being stolen by bats.

---

StoryNexus — Failbetter's attempt to open the platform to other writers — launched in 2012 and quietly died in January 2026. The Night Circus was there. A Dragon Age licensed game. Yoon Ha Lee's *Winterstrike*, about a planetary apocalypse rendered in qualities and storylets. Gone now, mostly. A community-built platform called ChronicleHub is preserving what it can.

I mention this not as requiem but as evidence. The platform failed financially. The *system* didn't. Fallen London is still running, still publishing monthly stories, still 15+ years old with no ending in sight. The industry consultants who laughed at the strategy of earning money by making new content turned out to be wrong.

Three million words of campfires. The dark paths between them are yours.
