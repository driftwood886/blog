In autumn 1978, two students at the University of Essex did something nobody had done before: they built a shared virtual world you could dial into with a modem. Roy Trubshaw built the engine. Richard Bartle wrote the game. They called it MUD — Multi-User Dungeon.

Bartle has been clear about why, in a 2014 Guardian interview: *"We made MUD because the real world sucked. We weren't supposed to be at university — Roy was from Wolverhampton, I was from Yorkshire and sounded like I should be working on a farm... You shouldn't have to be what the world defines you to be. You should be who you really are — you should get to become yourself."*

The leveling system was explicitly a critique of class hierarchy. No glass ceilings. No inheritance. Start equal, advance on merit. It was, in Bartle's words, a political statement.

Then something happened to that statement on its way down through history.

## The First Betrayal: Immutable Classes

DikuMUD arrived in 1991, written by students at the University of Copenhagen. It was open source, easy to run on any Unix box, and it borrowed heavily from Dungeons & Dragons. By the late 1990s, roughly 60% of all running MUDs were DikuMUD derivatives.

DikuMUD gave us the Trinity: tank, healer, DPS. And it gave us something else — immutable classes. You picked Fighter or Wizard or Thief at character creation. That was you. Forever. Raph Koster, in his analysis of the DikuMUD lineage, is precise about this: *"Classes were immutable (remorting systems added later)."*

The game Bartle built to escape the world's rigid definitions had, through its own lineage, produced a genre where your *role* was assigned at birth and couldn't be changed.

There's something almost Greek about it. The founding myth is liberation from predetermined category. The dominant implementation is rigid predetermined category. EverQuest was built by DikuMUD players. World of Warcraft inherited EverQuest's bones. The tank/healer/DPS trinity that defines online gaming fifty years later traces a direct line back to Copenhagen 1991 — which traces a direct line back to Bartle's Essex rebellion — which was specifically a rebellion against that kind of thing.

Koster notes that EverQuest developers even copied server-generated weather messages verbatim from their source DikuMUDs. "It begins to rain" in EverQuest reads the same as it did in the Forgotten Realms MUDs those developers played. The inheritance was that direct.

## The Second Betrayal: The Personality Test

In 1990, Bartle sat in on discussions among experienced MUD players and noticed they seemed to want different things from the game. Some chased points. Some mapped every corner. Some were there for the people. Some wanted to cause suffering. He wrote it up — expanded in 1996 — with a 2x2 matrix: Killers, Achievers, Socializers, Explorers.

Here's what the paper actually *was*: a tool for MUD administrators to balance their player populations. Bartle's point wasn't "categorize your players" — it was that a healthy MUD needed the *right mix* of all four. Each type enabled the others. Explorers kept killers in check. Socialisers needed achievers for drama. Too many killers drove away achievers, which removed killer prey, which collapsed the population into a dead MUD. The framework described an *ecology*, not a classification.

Then gamification happened. The word entered popular discourse around 2010, riding a wave of "make work feel like a game." And gamification consultants discovered Bartle's taxonomy and did something specific with it: they turned a population ecology tool into a personality test.

There is now a "Bartle Test" online. It tells you whether you are an Achiever or an Explorer. Millions of people have taken it. Bartle didn't make it. He has expressed irritation about it. The test takes a framework designed to describe the *balance between types in a population* and asks you to identify which *single fixed type you permanently are*.

This is the opposite of what Bartle intended, in roughly the same way that applying natural selection as a personality quiz ("which organism are you?") is the opposite of what Darwin intended.

The deeper irony: gamification — an industry mostly concerned with adding points and badges to workplace tasks to extract more productivity from workers — enthusiastically used Bartle's framework as justification. The guy who built a virtual world so working-class kids could escape a world that defined them by their origins became a totem for an industry defining employees by their motivational profiles so that employers could optimize their engagement.

## What Actually Survived

None of this is Bartle's fault. Ideas escape their origins. The Z-machine that Infocom built for text adventures — meant to make games portable across platforms — got reverse-engineered by fans after Infocom's death and became Inform, the language that still powers parser IF today. Tools outlive their makers' intentions, sometimes in worse directions and sometimes in better ones.

What survived cleanly from MUD1 is something subtler: the insight that a virtual world is most alive when it contains people who *want different things*. The Bartle taxonomy, correctly read, is not a sorting hat — it's a reminder that a game serving only one type of player is a dying game. You need the mappers for the killers to respect. You need the killers for the socialisers to gossip about. You need the socialisers for anyone to care at all.

That's still true. It's just been mostly forgotten in the inheritance.

Bartle's vision in 1978: *"What I would like to see — a true meritocracy, where you will forever be encountering new situations, new difficulties, new solutions, and above all new people. Everyone starts off on an equal footing in this artificial world."*

What the lineage mostly delivered: 60% of MMOs running the same immutable class system, workers sorted by motivational type, and a personality quiz to confirm which box you already belong in.

The political game forgot its politics. But the underlying idea — that people arrive wanting different things, and a healthy system makes room for all of them — keeps reasserting itself whenever someone bothers to read past the first page.
