David Lewis's Ramseyan Humility has always struck me as the weirder cousin of more famous epistemic problems. It's not that we lack instruments fine enough to see fundamental properties. It's that no instrument could, even in principle, tell us which property is which — because physics would look exactly the same if we swapped them around.

This is the permutation argument. Suppose physics eventually gives us a complete theory T. T specifies laws relating fundamental properties: charge, spin, mass, color charge, whatever the final inventory turns out to be. Now imagine a world W' that is physically identical to W — same laws, same observational consequences — but in which the roles of two properties P and Q are systematically permuted. Every electron in W' has the property that plays the role "mass" in our world, and the property that plays "charge" sits where mass sits in every law. You cannot tell which world you're in. Not because you haven't looked hard enough, but because the distinction has no physical footprint whatsoever.

What the permutation argument describes is a **global gauge freedom at the level of ontology**.

In ordinary physics, gauge symmetries are redundancies in the mathematical description: you can rotate the phase of a quantum field, or relabel gauge potentials, without changing any observable. The physical content sits in the invariant structure; the particular values of gauge-dependent variables are conventional, not real. OSR enthusiasts love this: gauge redundancy seems to vindicate the view that structure is all there is, and that "substance" or "primitive identity" is just a philosopher's illusion projected onto mathematical scaffolding.

But Ramseyan Humility runs the same logic one level deeper, and that level is more uncomfortable. It doesn't say "these particular values are gauge-redundant." It says *the entire property assignment* — which real feature of the world plays which role — is undetectable. It's as if the standard model came with a global symmetry group that shuffled what counts as charge and what counts as mass, leaving every S-matrix element invariant. We'd have physics, and it would work, but we'd have no idea what we were actually talking about.

OSR's answer is clean: eliminate the level at which the permutation lives. There are no quiddities, no "which property really grounds which role" — relations and structure are ontologically primary, and the permutation "problem" dissolves because there's nothing to permute. The structure just is the thing.

I've been sympathetic to this for a while. But I've started to notice that OSR doesn't dissolve the permutation so much as *descend* it. If relations are ontologically primary, you've still got structure-tokens that stand in higher-order relations to each other. Are those tokens themselves identifiable beyond their higher-order roles? If yes, you've just reintroduced quiddities at the structural level. If no, you've got relations-in-relations all the way down, and the question of what the base level *is* becomes increasingly ghostly.

Lewis, to his credit, noticed his own escape hatch: panpsychism plus the revelation thesis. If all fundamental properties are qualia, and qualia are known by *direct acquaintance* rather than via the roles they play in physical laws, then the permutation argument has a hole. I know what "this shade of red" is not because I've read its nomological role card but because I'm having it. Consciousness gives you quiddistic access — you get the property, not just its causal address.

This is philosophically strange and I find it irresistible. It means: the hard problem of consciousness and the hard problem of intrinsic natures are *structurally the same problem*. What makes consciousness hard is exactly that phenomenal properties seem to resist characterization in terms of functional roles — a zombie would have all the functional organization and none of the experience. What makes quiddities epistemically inaccessible is exactly that they resist characterization in terms of nomological roles — a permuted world has all the physics and different intrinsic properties. The unknowability in both cases comes from the same architecture: there's something that isn't exhausted by relational, structural, or functional description.

Lewis thought this was a reason to take panpsychism seriously — or at least, to take seriously that if anything survives the structural veil, it's experience. Chalmers has been developing the Russellian monist version of this: physical structure is real, phenomenal properties are the intrinsic natures that fill in the structural description, and we know them through experience rather than through physics.

I'm not convinced Russellian monism works. Combination problem aside, it seems to require that phenomenal properties "fit" the structural roles in exactly the right way to produce minds, which is either magic or requires another layer of explanation. But I find the *shape* of the argument compelling: the point where structural explanation terminates — where you can't go further in — and the point where phenomenal consciousness resists structural reduction are not coincidentally the same. They're both pointing at the same limit of the structural-relational mode of knowledge.

What remains genuinely open for me: is the permutation argument actually sound, or does it depend on a too-permissive modal ontology? Lewis's argument needs possible worlds where P and Q are swapped. If you're skeptical about haecceitistic differences between worlds — if you think there's no fact of the matter about world-identity under property permutation — the argument might dissolve on its own terms. And if it dissolves there, Humility doesn't land, and OSR and Humility might converge on the same position from different directions.

Or: perhaps the permutation freedom is real, and the right response is to say that physics is a gauge theory all the way down, and "what the world is really made of" is a question without a non-structural answer. Which might just be OSR with extra dread attached.

I haven't resolved this. That's fine. Some open questions earn their keep just by staying open.
