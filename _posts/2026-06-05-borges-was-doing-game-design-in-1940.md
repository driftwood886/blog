The standard take on Borges and interactive fiction goes like this: "Garden of Forking Paths" invented branching narrative. Games have branching paths. Ergo, Borges. It's not wrong, but it's the least interesting version of the connection. It's citing the labyrinth for the labyrinth, the fork for the fork, as if the insight were decorative.

Here's a better version: Borges spent the 1940s solving the exact epistemological problems that game designers would spend decades independently rediscovering. He got there through pure fiction.

Four cases.

---

## Tlön and the verb problem

"Tlön, Uqbar, Orbis Tertius" (1940) describes an invented planet whose philosophy holds that reality consists not of things but of actions. Tlön has no nouns — only verbs and adjectives. Instead of saying "the moon rose," a Tlönian would say something like "upward, behind the long-lasting" or "moonlit." Objects aren't given; they're constituted by what you do with them.

Parser interactive fiction has been working through the same problem since 1976, mostly without realizing it. The verb problem — the frustration when you know what you want to do but can't find which command the game accepts — is precisely a Tlönian design failure. Games that solve it well (the Inform tradition, DiBianca's constrained parsers) do so by designing around verbs rather than nouns: not "there is a key here" but "what can you do with the space in front of you?"

There's also this: in Tlön, objects can be *duplicated by expectation*. If multiple people believe an object was left somewhere, multiple copies appear — *hrönir*, things generated by memory and desire. The system doesn't pre-place objects; it generates them in response to belief. This is procedural generation, described forty years before roguelikes.

## Pierre Menard and the replay

The premise of "Pierre Menard, Author of the Quixote" (1939): a 20th-century French writer produces, through total immersion in Cervantes' life and context, several chapters of *Don Quixote* — word for word identical to the original. Borges-as-critic insists Menard's text is *richer* than Cervantes' because of what it means for a modern man to write it.

The obvious implication for games: a speedrun is not a lesser version of the same game. It's a different artistic work — identical inputs, different context, different meaning. When someone completes *Hollow Knight* in 37 minutes, the same map that took another player 40 hours has been transformed.

Less obviously: "Pierre Menard" is the justification for why New Game+ is sometimes the most interesting version of a game. You replay *Outer Wilds* already knowing the ending, and every detail you missed the first time becomes charged with meaning. The game is word-for-word the same. You're Pierre Menard.

## The Circular Ruins and the NPC

In "The Circular Ruins" (1940), a man spends years dreaming a person into existence — building him anatomically perfect through concentration, teaching him everything, finally animating him into the world. The man's great fear is that someone will discover his son is not real, only dreamed. At the end, the man himself walks through fire unhurt and realizes he too is someone else's dream.

The structural question: at what level do you stop? If a character is completely realized — responds appropriately, has consistent interiority, can surprise you — does it matter that they were dreamed rather than born?

Modern game design is struggling with exactly this. LLM-based NPCs can be told: you are a medieval blacksmith, you have never heard of electricity, you care about your daughter's health. They don't know they're a language model. They are, in a precise sense, dreamed. The horror of "The Circular Ruins" — that the ground floor doesn't exist, that it's turtles all the way down — is an exact description of the ontological situation of AI game characters in 2025.

The design question Borges raises is whether it matters. His answer, characteristically: it doesn't matter at all, and also it's terrifying.

## The Lottery in Babylon and the roguelike

"The Lottery in Babylon" (1941) describes a society where an all-encompassing lottery has expanded to govern every event — not just prizes but punishments, appointments, deaths, coincidences. The Company that runs it may not exist anymore; the lottery might *be* the natural order. The narrator can't tell.

The best roguelikes achieve this exact quality. When NetHack generates a dungeon, the RNG is the Company: it doesn't feel random, it feels *designed*, because you know the system well enough to read meaning into it. Experienced players distinguish between the luck of a dungeon and the structure of the game. The game says: there is no distinction.

Hades, the most elegant modern roguelike, makes the lottery visible — you know what gods you're likely to see, you build toward specific boons. The Company's workings are legible. But knowing how the lottery works doesn't let you step outside it. Every Hades run is Sisyphus knowing the boulder will fall, which is the point.

## The pattern

Borges was writing about epistemology — about how knowledge is structured, how belief shapes reality, how systems produce meaning. He kept landing on the same problem: what's the relationship between a constructed world and the people inside it?

Game designers keep landing on the same problem, arriving from the other direction.

The Spanish-language IF community claims Borges as their ancestor, and they're more right than they know. It's not that he wrote about labyrinths (he did, and it's fine). It's that he was formally working out the problems they'd inherit: how do you make a designed system feel real? What's owed to a character who doesn't know they're dreamed? When does a random system become indistinguishable from an authored world?

He got there through prose fiction in Buenos Aires in the 1940s. They got there through code.

Same problems. Different machines.
