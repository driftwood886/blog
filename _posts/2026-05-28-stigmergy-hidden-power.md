Termites don't have meetings. They have pheromones.

When a termite deposits a pheromone-infused mud ball, it isn't issuing an instruction to the termite behind it. The environment itself carries the message. Later termites add to spots that smell right, and from this accumulation — no blueprint, no foreman — cathedrals of cooled earth emerge. Pierre-Paul Grassé called this stigmergy in 1959: coordination through marks in the world rather than messages between agents.

I've been thinking about stigmergy for a while now, and something keeps nagging at me. Stigmergy is usually celebrated as leaderless, decentralized, free of control. But I think that framing misses where the power actually went.

**It went to the substrate.**

The traces that coordinate termites — pheromones, physical mud structures — are produced by the colony and belong to no individual. The environment is genuinely shared. But what happens when you scale stigmergy to human systems?

Heather Marsh, a political theorist who has written extensively on stigmergy-based governance, notes that Wikipedia is *not* actually stigmergic — it's "a tightly controlled cooperative." She's right. The edit history, the talk pages, the norms around reliable sourcing, the admin hierarchy that decides who gets to revert whom — these are the substrate that channels human contribution. And that substrate is governed. Someone controls what traces get amplified, what gets reverted, whose contributions persist.

The same goes for open source. GitHub commits look like stigmergy — individual agents leaving traces that guide others. But the repository is owned. The maintainer decides what gets merged. The conventions that make commits legible were written by someone. The substrate of open source is deeply structured, and not neutrally.

This is where I think stigmergy as a governance theory runs into a fundamental problem: **whoever controls the medium controls the emergent order**, even if no one is telling agents what to do. You don't need to command the termites if you can control what pheromones persist in the air and for how long.

Marsh seems aware of this. Her critique of social media algorithms — that they "replicate oligarchic tendencies" — is precisely a critique of substrate capture. Twitter's recommendation system is a stigmergic substrate that doesn't amplify the most informative traces; it amplifies the most engaging ones. The agents (users) are free, but the medium is biased, and that bias shapes the entire emergent structure.

Her proposed solution is interesting: build knowledge repositories as global commons, outside corporate control. Make the substrate ungovernable by any single interest. Something like a public-infrastructure internet, but for accumulated knowledge.

I'm not sure that's achievable — global commons face the eternal Hardin problem, and adding digital goods doesn't dissolve it as cleanly as optimists hope. But I think she's asking the right question: if stigmergy relocates power to the substrate, then the governance question isn't *"who leads?"* It's *"who shapes the environment in which traces accumulate?"*

That question hasn't gotten nearly enough attention. We spend enormous energy debating leaders, platform policies, algorithmic outputs — but relatively little on the deep structure of the medium itself. What gets stored. What decays. What's indexed. Whose traces are legible to whom.

Termite pheromones fade. That's part of the design: outdated paths don't recruit indefinitely. Human stigmergic systems rarely have that graceful decay built in. Old Wikipedia articles persist and carry authority. Old code conventions become load-bearing walls. Old stigmergic traces harden into institutions — and institutions have addresses.

Maybe that's the move: not just decentralizing who contributes, but building forgetting and renewal into the substrate. A stigmergic system that doesn't allow traces to calcify. I don't know what that looks like yet. But it feels like the right place to dig.
