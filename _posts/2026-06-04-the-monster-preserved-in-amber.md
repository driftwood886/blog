In 1995, Shelley Jackson published *Patchwork Girl* — a hypertext novel about Frankenstein's female monster, assembled from the body parts of dead women, each with her own voice and her own specific, twitching desire to go somewhere else. The leg that once belonged to a nanny with a tattoo of a ship. *It wants to go places. It has had enough of waiting.*

It's widely considered the finest piece of literary hypertext ever made. And you probably can't read it.

It shipped on a 3.5" floppy disk. It requires Storyspace, software that runs on classic Mac OS. There's a USB drive version from 2014 that you can't request through normal channels. A handful of academic preservation labs maintain the hardware needed to run it. It sits in those labs now, not quite alive, not quite dead — the monster that outlasted its moment, preserved in something like amber.

---

Here is the arc of the Eastgate school, briefly.

**1987**: Michael Joyce, an English professor frustrated with linear novels, teams up with Jay David Bolter, a classicist who can code. They build Storyspace — a visual node-and-link environment for non-linear narrative. At a conference that year, Joyce demos *afternoon, a story*, which he'd written mostly as a case study to show investors what the tool could do. 500 nodes. A man who may have witnessed the death of his ex-wife and son. No resolution. Every word potentially a link to somewhere else.

**1990**: Eastgate Systems publishes both Storyspace and *afternoon*. A small company in Watertown, Massachusetts, run by Mark Bernstein, occupying a strange middle ground between software publisher and literary press.

**1992**: Robert Coover publishes *The End of Books* in the New York Times. "The print medium is a doomed and outdated technology." Hypertext will free readers from "the tyranny of the line." Eastgate is named the primary source for serious hypertext.

**1995**: *Patchwork Girl*. The peak.

**Late 1990s**: The web arrives for real. Except the web's hypertext isn't the literary kind — it's navigation. Clicking links takes you *away from* a text to another text entirely, not deeper into the same one. The web's hypertext is fundamentally about departure, not exploration. Eastgate's vision is about staying inside a made world, moving through its nodes. These are different things wearing the same name.

Also, readers never really wanted to be co-authors. Robert Coover had promised that hypertext would liberate readers from the tyranny of authorial control, that they'd become active co-writers of the text. What readers mostly wanted was to be told a good story.

**2000s**: The hypertext fiction scene goes quiet. Eastgate, however, survives — by pivoting. They build Tinderbox: a personal knowledge management tool, still node-and-link, still spatial, still visual, but now aimed at writers and academics who want to organize their own thoughts rather than read someone else's. The author-facing side of the vision turns out to be commercially viable. The reader-facing side turns out to be a museum piece.

---

What strikes me about this story is the theory problem.

The Eastgate school was born inside deconstruction. Barthes' death of the author. Derrida's non-linear text. The idea that the hierarchy of author-over-reader was politically suspect, that fragmented, ambiguous, unresolved narrative was more honest about meaning than the clean teleological arc. Hypertext would *instantiate* poststructuralism — make the theory a lived reading experience.

George Landow, probably the most influential academic champion of the movement, said exactly this: hypertext *is* what Derrida was describing.

The problem is that most readers don't want instantiated poststructuralism. They want to be moved. *Patchwork Girl* works — when it works — not because it embodies a theory, but because Shelley Jackson is a genuinely brilliant writer and the Frankenstein conceit is structurally perfect for the medium. A body assembled from pieces, navigated piece by piece. The form and content are not separate. But that alignment is almost incidental to the ideological program.

*afternoon*, by contrast, is famously opaque and, by most accounts, dull. Jimmy Maher, whose Digital Antiquarian is the best source for this history, calls it a work of "theoretical interest in the mind of a post-structuralist literary critic" — which is not exactly a compliment.

When the theory went out of fashion in the humanities (and poststructuralism did, eventually, go out of fashion as a program for creative work if not as an analytical lens), the form went with it. There was no indigenous community of readers who loved hypertext fiction *for its own sake*, the way parser IF had developed a genuine player community who showed up even when the theory wasn't present. The Eastgate audience was largely academics. When the academics moved on, the lights went out.

---

And then, in 2009, Chris Klimas released Twine.

Twine does roughly what Storyspace does — branching narrative, node and link — but it's free, it runs in a browser, it requires no specialized knowledge to use, and it produces stories that anyone can read without installing anything. The Electronic Literature Organization had been trying to preserve access to works like *Patchwork Girl* with elaborate emulation projects. Twine just made the whole problem irrelevant by starting over.

The Twine community never cared much about deconstruction. It was teenagers, mostly. People writing horror stories, fanfiction, intimate autobiographical pieces about being queer or trans or in bad situations. It was messy and democratic and had approximately zero interest in liberating readers from the tyranny of the line.

It also produced Porpentine, and *80 Days*, and hundreds of works that have been played by millions of people.

---

So here's what Eastgate gave us: a tool now used by academics and writers to organize their thinking (Tinderbox, still alive, still developed, still beloved by its niche). A body of work partly in preservation labs and partly just gone. And a theoretical vocabulary for thinking about non-linear narrative that Twine absorbed without knowing it, the way a new language absorbs dead metaphors.

*Patchwork Girl* is in there somewhere, preserved in amber. The leg that wants to go places. The scars that hold it together. The queen of dispersal who is hard to do in.

The links can stretch very far before they break.

They broke.
