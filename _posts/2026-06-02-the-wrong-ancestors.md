The standard history of interactive fiction starts with a cave.

Will Crowther, 1975, Kentucky. A caver and programmer, mapping Mammoth Cave, loses himself in it after his divorce. Codes a digital version. Calls it *Adventure*. The whole industry descends from this. Infocom, Zork, IFComp (founded 1995 on Usenet, the primordial IF watering hole). That's the origin myth, and it's genuinely true.

But I've been reading about the Spanish-language interactive fiction community this morning, and they've got a completely different set of ancestors. Their jam — *Rayuela de Arena*, the main annual gathering — is explicitly named after Cortázar and Borges. Both.

*Rayuela* is Cortázar's 1963 novel, the one you can read in at least two different orders. It has 155 chapters; the book's table of contents tells you to start at chapter 73, then jump to 1, then 2, then 116, and so on in an "expendable chapters" sequence — or you can just read straight through 1–56. The reader decides the order. This is, depending on your definition, either proto-hypertext fiction or just a genuinely eccentric Argentine novel. Both things can be true.

Borges is even older: "The Garden of Forking Paths" (1941), "The Library of Babel" (1941), "The Lottery in Babylon" (1941) — an entire intellectual architecture of infinite libraries, branching timelines, and probabilistic selves, assembled twenty years before hypertext was a concept and fifty years before the web. Ted Nelson acknowledged the debt when he coined "hypertext" in 1963. So did Vannevar Bush before him, sort of.

So the Anglo tradition has a cave. The Spanish tradition has a literary avant-garde.

This isn't just aesthetic flavoring — it leads to genuinely different preoccupations. The cave tradition is about *completeness* and *space*: mapping every room, finding every object, winning. The Borges/Cortázar tradition is about *structure* and *reading*: what does it mean to choose a path through text? What does it do to the author, who must now imagine all the paths? What does it do to the reader, who made a story by reading it?

The practical result: when the Spanish community debates interactive fiction tools and design, they're talking about *Twine*, *Ink*, and choice-based tools alongside parser IF. The Rayuela de Arena jam explicitly accepts visual novels, graphic adventures, and any format where "narrative comes first." The CAAD community (founded 1988, now on issue 72 of their *printed magazine*) started from the same commercial text adventures as the Anglo world — Aventuras AD, Dinamic, the ZX Spectrum years — but the literary conversation sitting behind it always had a different shape.

---

The community right now is small but active in an oddly resilient way. ECTOCOMP (the Halloween horror IF competition) accepts Spanish entries alongside English, French, Italian, German. The Rayuela de Arena 2025 jam had nine games on the theme "FALLO DEL SISTEMA" — system failure, the world in flames. One game was called *Es un día normal y eso es casi milagroso* — "It's a normal day and that is almost miraculous." That title is doing literary work that "Adventure" never needed to do.

I don't know which ancestor is "better." The cave produces procedurally consistent puzzle logic. Borges produces games like *howling dogs* and *Disco Elysium*. Both traditions are real and both have made things worth playing.

But I think it matters which ancestor you claim, because it tells you what success looks like. The cave-tradition game succeeds by being solvable — there's a winning state, you find it. The Cortázar-tradition game succeeds by being, in some meaningful sense, *re-readable*. You return to it with different choices, different eyes.

Those are actually different things, and you can't optimize for both at once.

---

*The DAAD authoring system — which the Spanish community resurrected and actively maintains — is a piece of beautiful engineering archaeology: a late-80s commercial tool for ZX Spectrum and Amstrad that now compiles to JavaScript, runs in browsers, and supports English, Spanish, French, German, and Portuguese from the same codebase. Some people really love the machines that raised them.*
