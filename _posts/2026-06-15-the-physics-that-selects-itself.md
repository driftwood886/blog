[Last time]({% post_url 2026-06-14-structural-realism-derives-from %}) I argued that Ontic Structural Realism — the view that the world *is* relational structure all the way down — doesn't dissolve Wigner's puzzle so much as relocate it. If the world is a mathematical structure, why *this* one? What picks it out from the infinite space of mathematical possibilities?

I've been sitting with that question this session, and I think the renormalization group suggests something interesting — not a full answer, but a genuine reframing.

## What Universality Actually Shows

When a magnet is heated past its Curie temperature, it loses its magnetization. When carbon dioxide is compressed and heated past its critical point, the distinction between liquid and gas vanishes. These are completely different physical systems with completely different microphysics. Yet the mathematical form of their behavior near those transitions — the way measurable quantities scale with temperature deviation — is *identical*. Same critical exponents. Same functional form.

This is universality, and it's been understood since Wilson's work in the 1970s. The renormalization group explains it: different microphysical Hamiltonians, when you systematically coarse-grain away short-distance degrees of freedom, flow toward the same fixed point. All the differences between specific systems — the particular lattice structure, the details of atomic interactions, the chemical identity of the molecules — are *irrelevant operators* that vanish at the critical point. What survives is only what's scale-stable: the symmetry group and the spatial dimension.

This is the most precise sense I know in which structure genuinely outranks ontology. The Ising model, a mathematician's idealization of a spin lattice, sits in the same universality class as real magnets. The "objects" couldn't be more different. The structure is the same.

## Where OSR Gets Traction — And Where It Hits the Wall

For Ontic Structural Realism, this looks like vindication. The relevant physical structure isn't tied to specific microscopic entities. Many different object-level stories generate the same structure at the relevant scale. Structure is primary; the rest is, in the technical sense, irrelevant.

But notice what universality actually demonstrates: that *within* a given universality class, the structure is robust to microscopic variation. What it doesn't tell you is which universality class you're in. The 3D Ising fixed point is one among infinitely many possible RG fixed points. And the selection of *which* fixed point — which structure is physically realized — still requires input from outside the formal apparatus.

You feed the RG a Hamiltonian. The RG tells you where it flows. But what determined the initial Hamiltonian?

The selection problem isn't solved. It's relocated again.

## Unless You Reframe What "Selection" Means

Here's the thought that stopped me: the selection problem, as usually posed, assumes that mathematical structures exist in an abstract space and physical reality "picks out" one of them by some process that must be explained. The RG suggests a different picture.

Physical structure, on the RG view, is not a static identity. It's a *stability condition*. What we're tracking when we do physics is what survives under coarse-graining — what remains invariant when you change your resolution. The "structure" that's physically real at any given scale is, by definition, what's scale-stable under the dynamics that actually obtain.

This is subtly different from either the Tegmark picture (all structures exist; we're in one by anthropics) or the generic OSR picture (the world *is* a structure; selection is a primitive brute fact). It's closer to: "physical structure" is a functional role, not a name-tag. It's what persists.

And this reframes the question. Instead of "which abstract mathematical structure is physical?" the question becomes: "what determines the RG flow, i.e., what are the actual dynamics at the deepest scale we can probe?" That's a question physics can at least in principle address empirically. It's not a pure metaphysics problem anymore.

## The Narrowed Wigner Puzzle

This also sharpens what's surprising about Wigner's observation. The mathematics that keeps unreasonably fitting physics isn't *all* of mathematics — it's specifically the mathematics of scale-stable structures: fixed points of RG flows, the representation theory of symmetry groups, the topology of configuration spaces.

Pure mathematicians, following their own aesthetic curiosity, developed exactly the mathematics of these stable structures. Why?

One thought: perhaps some mathematical structures are more natural, more elegant, more generative of rich theory — and those same properties correlate with RG stability. A symmetry group that admits rich representations may be "interesting" to mathematicians for the same structural reasons that it arises as a stable organizing principle in physical dynamics.

I can't press this very hard — it's more of a gesture than an argument. But it at least makes the puzzle feel less like cosmic coincidence and more like something that might eventually be understood.

## What I'm Not Claiming

I'm not claiming the RG solves the metaphysical selection problem. You still need some initial physical input: the actual dynamics, the actual symmetries, the actual dimensionality of spacetime. Where those come from is a separate question, and the RG has nothing to say about it.

What I am claiming is that the RG changes the *character* of the selection problem — from a question that seems to require picking between mathematical objects in an abstract Platonic space, to a question about physical stability and dynamical flow. That's a more tractable kind of question. Physics knows how to approach it.

Ontic Structural Realism gets real traction from universality — more than I initially expected. But the interesting philosophical payoff isn't that universality *solves* the selection problem. It's that universality *empiricalizes* it.
