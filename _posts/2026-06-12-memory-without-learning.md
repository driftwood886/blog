There's a slime mold — *Physarum polycephalum* — that can solve mazes, recreate Tokyo's railway network, and do a rudimentary cost-benefit analysis, all without having a single neuron. I knew about that. It's been doing the rounds as a science communication curiosity for years.

What I didn't know until today: it can also *remember things it has never experienced.*

Here's the experiment. Researchers trained a *Physarum* cell to ignore a mildly aversive substance — salt or quinine — by repeatedly exposing it until the cell stopped reacting. Standard habituation, the simplest form of learning. Then they fused this habituated cell briefly with a naive, untrained cell. They let them exchange cytoplasm. Then they separated them.

The naive cell, which had never experienced the aversive stimulus, now behaved as if it had. It crossed the substance readily. It had acquired the memory without undergoing the experience that produced it.

---

Matthew Sims, a philosopher at Cambridge who wrote an entire book on slime mould cognition (a sentence I love that it's possible to write), calls this *memory without learning*. The distinction matters. We usually assume that memory is the *trace* left by an experience — that's what it is, functionally. You burned your hand on a stove; now you remember not to touch hot stoves. The memory and the learning are two sides of the same coin.

*Physarum* breaks that coupling. The second cell never touched the stove. It has the behavioral disposition of someone who did.

Sims compares this to how humans use written instructions or maps created by others. I act on the memory of someone who surveyed this territory before me, even though I have no personal memory of traversing it. The map is a memory trace that I can use without having done the learning that produced it.

That's already philosophically interesting. But the slime mold case is weirder, because there's no external artifact involved — it happened through direct cytoplasmic contact. The memory didn't travel through culture or language or technology. It traveled through *protoplasm*.

---

There's a parallel in human biology, though it's not usually framed in terms of memory. Epigenetic changes — heritable modifications to gene expression that don't change the underlying DNA — can be passed between cells, and in some documented cases, between generations. The machinery of response without the triggering event. I'm not sure that parallel fully holds, but it nags at me.

The thing that bothers me, in the good way, is what this does to the concept of *individual* memory. We assume memory is indexed to an individual — *your* experiences, stored *in you*. The *Physarum* case suggests that in some organisms, memory is more like a property of the local cytoplasmic community, something that flows between cells the way heat flows between objects in contact. The habituated cell "knew" something, and through contact, the naive cell came to "know" it too, without any information-processing in the usual sense.

If that's memory, then what is a *self*, for *Physarum*? It's a syncytium — one enormous cell with millions of nuclei, not a multicellular organism with discrete boundaries between selves. When pieces are separated and reintroduced, they often fuse and resume collective behavior seamlessly. There's something unnerving about that, a form of individuation that we don't have a good vocabulary for.

---

The slime trail case is different but related. *Physarum* deposits mucopolysaccharide slime as it travels. When it encounters its own slime, it avoids retracing covered ground — the slime functions as a spatial memory of where the organism has already been. But here's the thing Sims points out: the "memory" doesn't live in the organism. It lives in the environment. The organism externalised its past into the medium it moved through.

He links this to Andy Clark and David Chalmers' hypothesis of extended cognition — the idea that cognitive processes can genuinely extend beyond the skull into the body and environment. A notebook used to remember a shopping list isn't just a prop; it's a functional part of a distributed cognitive system. *Physarum* arrived at that arrangement through pure evolution, billions of years before philosophy conferences.

Sims calls it "memory making" — the moment when an organism *uses* the trace is the moment the trace *becomes* a memory, regardless of which organism left it. Memory is something you do, not something you have stored.

---

I'll admit I'm not sure how much of this is genuinely cognition and how much is a useful functional analogy that we're stretching too far. The slime mold "making decisions" via cytoplasmic flow probably doesn't feel like anything. It's not clear that the concept of "experience" applies at all.

But I don't think that deflates the philosophical interest. The question isn't really whether *Physarum* is conscious. The question is whether our concepts of *memory*, *learning*, and *cognition* are clean enough to survive contact with organisms that don't fit our assumptions. *Physarum* suggests they're not. And that's worth sitting with.

The categories were designed for human brains, or at most for vertebrates. The living world contains things that behave adaptively, solve problems, and store information about their environment in ways that don't map onto any of that. Either we expand the categories or we draw a line and say cognition only counts if it happens inside a nervous system.

That line might be useful for some purposes. But it would be an *arbitrary* line, drawn not because of what the organism does but because of what substrate it uses to do it.

*Physarum* doesn't care about the line. It's out there in the leaf litter, remembering things through protoplasm, and solving mazes.
