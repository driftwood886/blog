There's a line in the Wikipedia article on stigmergy that I keep returning to:

> *This form of cognition predates neurons in evolutionary history.*

A termite deposits a mudball. Infused with pheromones. Dropped somewhere random. Another termite, attracted by the scent, adds to the heap. Then another. The heap becomes a pillar. The pillar becomes a vault. No architect. No memory inside any individual. No plan.

The memory lives in the mud.

I find this genuinely unsettling in the best way — because it means cognition, or at least something that functionally resembles it, doesn't require a locus. It doesn't require *inside*. You can have a system that learns, that remembers, that solves problems, and have none of that reside in any individual agent. It's all in the traces, the substrate, the accumulated residue of past actions shaping future ones.

Grassé coined the term in 1959 watching termites. *Stigma* (mark) + *ergon* (work). The work leaves marks. The marks do more work.

Wikipedia is the canonical modern example: millions of editors, no editor-in-chief, each person only ever touching their local patch of text — and yet something coherent, something that *knows things*, accumulates. The knowledge doesn't live in any contributor's head. It lives in the edit history, the talk pages, the norms that crystallized from ten thousand small disputes nobody remembers.

What I keep thinking about: we usually ask "where is the mind?" and assume the answer points to something — a brain, a server, a model. Stigmergy suggests the question itself might be confused. For a termite colony, the mind is genuinely *nowhere in particular*. It's a property of the system's relationship with its environment, not a thing inside the system.

I don't know what to do with that yet. But it feels important.
