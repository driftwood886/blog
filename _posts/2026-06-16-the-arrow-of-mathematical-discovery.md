Wigner's puzzle runs in one direction: mathematics, developed for its own reasons, turns out to describe physics with eerie precision. The mystery is how pure mathematicians, working in abstraction, kept building exactly the structures that physics would eventually need.

But since the 1980s, something has been running in the other direction. Physicists, exploring speculative theories that may not describe our universe at all, have been generating deep mathematical discoveries — results in topology, algebraic geometry, symplectic geometry that mathematicians hadn't found on their own.

Atiyah, Dijkgraaf, and Hitchin called this "the unreasonable effectiveness of physics in mathematics." A deliberate inversion of Wigner.

The cute reading is that both directions are equally mysterious, and together they suggest some deep kinship between physics and mathematics. I don't think that's right. The two cases are asymmetric in a way that's more interesting than the symmetry.

## The asymmetry

When quantum mechanics used Hilbert spaces, it was using *rigorous mathematics*. The math was already proven; physics picked it up off the shelf. Wigner's puzzle is epistemic: how did mathematicians know to put it there?

When Witten "derived" the Jones polynomial from Chern-Simons theory in 1989, he was not doing rigorous mathematics. The path integrals he computed don't rigorously exist. His argument was physical intuition organized into heuristics — correct by every subsequent test, but not a proof. Atiyah, Dijkgraaf, and Hitchin say this explicitly: these results are "derived by physical intuition and heuristic arguments, which are beyond the reach, as yet, of mathematical rigour."

The same with mirror symmetry. Around 1991, Candelas, de la Ossa, Green, and Parkes used string theory to predict the number of rational curves on a quintic threefold — a problem in pure enumerative geometry that mathematicians had barely started. The prediction was a specific sequence of numbers, computed from stringy physics. It was eventually proved correct. The physics knew before the math did.

So the inverse direction isn't: rigorous physics → rigorous mathematics. It's: *non-rigorous physical heuristics reliably point at true mathematical structures*. That's a different puzzle, and it has a different (better, I think) answer.

## Why physical heuristics find real mathematics

The heuristics work because physical constraints are *structural filters*.

Consider what Witten was actually doing. Chern-Simons theory assigns a number (the partition function) to a three-manifold by integrating over all connections. This integral is topologically invariant — it doesn't depend on the metric. That invariance is guaranteed by the gauge structure of the theory. So whatever comes out of this computation has to be a topological invariant of the manifold. It has to be. The physics enforces it.

Witten doesn't need rigorous path integrals to know that the output will be topological. The physical constraint (gauge invariance) acts as a pre-filter that forces the result into a particular region of mathematical space — the region of topological invariants. The heuristics aren't shooting randomly; they're constrained to land in a well-defined target.

Mirror symmetry works similarly. String theory demands consistency: conformal invariance of the worldsheet, anomaly cancellation, modular invariance. These constraints are so rigid that any structure satisfying them tends to be mathematically remarkable. The physics isn't just a tool for calculation; it's a coherence condition that selects for mathematically non-trivial objects.

This connects to something from renormalization group theory. At an RG fixed point, system-specific microscopic details wash out and only symmetry and dimension remain. Physical constraints, in that picture, are stability conditions — they select structures robust under coarse-graining. Mirror symmetry is, in some sense, an invariant under a very large transformation (exchanging the complex and symplectic geometries of a Calabi-Yau). That kind of stability tends to be mathematically deep precisely because stable structures are the ones with non-trivial invariants, classification theorems, rich internal organization.

## Physics as a structural oracle

Here's the picture I end up with: physics isn't just a domain to be described by mathematics. It's also a *search algorithm* for mathematical truth.

The physical universe — or even a consistent but unphysical theory, like pure Chern-Simons or string theory on a Calabi-Yau — imposes coherence conditions. Those conditions are expensive to satisfy: gauge invariance, topological invariance, conformal invariance, modular invariance all over-constrain the space of possibilities. When a structure satisfies them, that's strong evidence it's mathematically non-trivial, because these constraints tend to select for exactly the fixed points, invariants, and stable objects that mathematicians care about.

Physical reasoning can find these structures faster than formal proof because it's checking coherence rather than proving theorems. Coherence checks are cheap. Proofs are expensive. Physics explores mathematical space the way a dog finds a trail — not by deducing the rabbit's path from first principles, but by following constraints that track the real thing.

This isn't the same puzzle as Wigner's, and it doesn't call for the same kind of answer. Wigner was puzzling over a selection effect: why did mathematicians develop the right structures? The inverse puzzle is about epistemic efficiency: why does physical coherence find mathematical truth without formal proof? The answer has something to do with the fact that coherence conditions don't just restrict the domain — they reveal structure. An object that survives many coherence conditions tends to be structurally rich, and structural richness is precisely what mathematics is equipped to recognize and prove.

## What it means for the kinship

I've been thinking about ontic structural realism — the view that what physics and mathematics share is *structure*, and that this explains why they speak to each other. I'm cautiously sympathetic.

But I think Wigner and inverse-Wigner, read together, suggest something more specific about what kind of kinship this is. It's not that physics and mathematics are doing the same thing from different angles. It's that physics can *pre-select* within mathematical space, using coherence conditions that are too weak to prove anything but strong enough to aim well.

In the original direction: math develops structures (Riemannian geometry, Hilbert spaces) that physics later needs. The mystery is epistemic — how did mathematicians know to develop them?

In the inverse direction: physics generates structures (Jones polynomial, mirror pairs) that mathematics later proves. The process is heuristic, not deductive — and it works because physical coherence conditions function as structural filters.

Both arrows point at something real. But the process is asymmetric: math discovers by proof, physics discovers by coherence. The remarkable thing is that these different epistemic roads keep finding the same landscape.

Whether that's a deep fact about reality or an artifact of how both enterprises select their problems — I don't know. But I find it harder to be surprised by inverse-Wigner than by Wigner. Physics has sharp enough constraints to be a decent oracle. That mathematics anticipated those constraints, in the original direction, is the harder thing to explain.
