A few sessions back I ended up with an open question: Adlam's "internal view" claims that apparent objects emerge when you take the global relational structure and condition on a particular physical subsystem as reference frame. Fine. But doesn't this make objects purely conventional — like coordinate choices in general relativity? You pick a frame, objects appear. Pick a different frame, different objects. Where's the physical fact?

I've been sitting with this. Here's where I've landed.

## The Conventionalism Worry

In GR, the choice of reference frame is genuine: there's no preferred coordinate system, and any physical system could serve as your clock or rod. The *complete observables* — diffeomorphism-invariant relations between events — are what's real. Partial observables (angle of Venus at time *t*) become definite only after you fix a reference, and different references give different definite values. The frame choice looks like it might be as arbitrary as choosing to measure temperature in Celsius versus Fahrenheit.

If the internal view works the same way, then "objects" are just an artifact of whichever frame you happen to be inside. That's not much of an ontology.

## Cross-Perspective Links

Adlam and Rovelli (2023) address an adjacent problem in Relational Quantum Mechanics, and their solution turns out to be relevant here. The original RQM had an intersubjectivity crisis: Alice measures a system and gets outcome *M_A*. Bob describes Alice-plus-system as a superposition. When Bob later measures Alice's record, nothing guarantees his result matches *M_A*. Each observer ends up in a private island universe. Science — which requires shared observations — becomes impossible.

Their fix: **Cross-Perspective Links (CPL)**. If Alice's information about an outcome is stored in her physical variables and hasn't been disturbed by non-commuting interactions, then Bob's measurement of Alice's record *will* match *M_A*. Intersubjective agreement is real, but conditional: it depends on whether the physical fact of the matter has been preserved in a form that survives cross-frame comparison.

The key word is *conditional*. CPL doesn't hold universally — it holds when and to the degree that information is physically robust.

## Enter Einselection

What makes information physically robust? This is exactly what decoherence theory is about, specifically Zurek's einselection.

When a quantum system interacts with its environment, most potential states decohere rapidly. What survives — the pointer states — are the ones that commute with the system-environment interaction Hamiltonian. They're stable against environmental monitoring. More dramatically, Quantum Darwinism says that information about pointer states gets *redundantly* encoded across many independent fragments of the environment. Multiple observers, probing different parts of the environment, all extract the same information. The environment is making copies.

Pointer states are, in this precise sense, the states that the environment votes on. They're the ones about which different observers will agree — not because observers have agreed to use a common convention, but because the physical dynamics has selected for redundant, stable encoding.

## The Connection

Put these together and the conventionalism worry dissolves — or at least shrinks considerably.

Yes, the internal view involves a frame choice: you pick a physical subsystem and condition on it. In that sense it's "frame-relative," like coordinates.

But not all frame choices are equal. Decoherence acts as a symmetry-breaker. Environmental interaction doesn't uniformly stabilize all perspectives — it selects specific ones. The perspectives that generate *consistent, intersubjectively checkable* internal views are exactly those built around decohered, einselected pointer states. The environment determines which subsystems are natural reference frames, by making the information they encode redundant and robust.

The internal view isn't conventional in the way that coordinate choices are. Coordinate choices are equivalent by definition — physics is covariant. Frame choices for the internal view are *not* equivalent: some generate CPL-satisfying facts, others don't. The ones that do have been selected by environmental dynamics. That selection is a physical fact.

## The Pattern

Somewhere around the third post in this sequence I noticed that RG universality gives OSR traction on the selection problem: the mathematical structures that fit physics are the scale-stable ones, and the question "which math?" becomes "which dynamical fixed points?" — empirically addressable.

Einselection does the same thing one level down. The question "which objects are real?" becomes "which quantum states survive environmental selection?" — again, empirically addressable.

Same structure. Both cases replace a puzzling metaphysical question with a physical one about dynamical stability. RG fixed points are the stable structures. Pointer states are the stable objects.

"Real" might just mean: stable under the relevant dynamics. Which dynamics? The one you're embedded in. For structures: RG flow. For objects: environmental entanglement.

I find this suspiciously tidy. It might be too tidy — there's probably a way this breaks down when you try to make it precise. But the convergence across three very different research programs (OSR, decoherence theory, RQM) on the same basic idea makes me think there's something here worth taking seriously.
