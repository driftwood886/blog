In late 2012, a young trans woman named Porpentine — recently kicked out of home at 14, still figuring out how to survive — started hormone replacement therapy and immediately sat down and wrote a game. She wrote it in a week. It was called *Howling Dogs*.

*Howling Dogs* opens with a quote from Nobel laureate Kenzaburō Ōe about "the dignity of human beings." The player is imprisoned in a small metal room with no explanation. An LCD counter ticks up each day. The only escape is a simulation rig that transports you into virtual worlds — which turn out to be other prisons. A woman awaiting immolation for believing herself something no woman was allowed to be. A companion on a moor whose captor's oppression is so complete that murder becomes the only available narrative, regardless of what you choose. A child empress who has spent her life learning the proper ways to die.

The writing is ferocious: *"the stones wonder if it is interesting to suffer."* The game didn't place in the top ten of the IF Competition that year. It won the Golden Banana of Discord — the award for greatest spread between high and low scores. One reviewer wrote: *"It was confusing, it was confronting, and as Art it succeeded in unbalancing me."*

The same year, Porpentine published a manifesto: "Creation Under Capitalism and the Twine Revolution." In it she described parser interactive fiction — the classic command-line tradition of *Adventure* and *Zork* — as "an invisible god figure that punishes you for failing to understand, as a representation of the smirking nerd, the obnoxious dungeon master." She described games as mostly built around "colleges, mansions, middle-class homes, generic fantasy worlds." She wrote: *"I'm not college, I'm not mansions. What is that to me?"*

And then: *"Twine is the closest we've come to a blank page."*

---

Twine had been invented three years earlier by a Baltimore web developer named Chris Klimas. It was free, browser-based, and its core syntax was almost embarrassingly simple:

```
You are in a room with two doors. You can either enter [[the door on the left]] or [[the door on the right]].
```

That's it. No parser vocabulary to learn. No legitimate actions to intuit. No system asking you to figure out what verbs it understood. Just a sentence with a link in it.

In one sense this was technically impoverished. Parser IF could model objects, rooms, inventories, conversations. Twine couldn't — or at least, it started from a much more minimal baseline. A lot of people in the IF community responded accordingly, dismissing Twine games as not really *games*, or not really *interactive fiction*, or not really worthy of serious consideration.

But Porpentine understood something those critics missed: **the parser's vocabulary was never neutral.** It encoded assumptions about whose actions counted, what questions were legible, who got to ask. The "invisible dungeon master" wasn't some absent god of neutral challenge — it was a specific cultural construct, built by specific people for specific people. When it punished you for saying the wrong thing, it wasn't testing cleverness impartially. It was testing fluency in a particular dialect.

The blank page was different. Not better in every dimension — more limited in some. But honest about its limits. You could see every option available to you; the link was right there. And crucially: the writer's vocabulary *became* the game's vocabulary. If Porpentine wanted to write "the stones wonder if it is interesting to suffer" — there was no system to reject that. No parser to correct her diction. The game was whatever she could say.

---

By 2013, this had become a movement. Anna Anthropy — trans woman, prolific designer, author of *The Rise of the Videogame Zinesters* — had been one of the first indie developers to discover Twine, and she described it at GDC 2013 as *"an amazing queer and woman-orientated game-making community that didn't even exist a year ago."* Merritt Kopas, writing from inside the community: *"For most of my adult life I had assumed that there were no games being made by queer people for queer people... Someone can just make a game like they would make a poem or a sketch. That's really powerful."*

The games made in this period are startling in their range: *Dys4ia* (Anna Anthropy's autobiographical account of hormone therapy), *Queers in Love at the End of the World* (a two-minute game about everything you'd do if you knew the world was ending), *Depression Quest* (a game about depression that crosses out options available to people who aren't depressed, as a design mechanic). None of these could have been made in parser IF — not because the technology didn't permit it, but because the culture didn't permit it. The cost of entry wasn't just financial, though that was real too: Anna Anthropy couldn't afford the $95 IGF entry fee; a friend paid it. Game Maker, one of the alternative engines, had shifted from free to $100. Twine cost nothing. The blank page was free.

---

Then GamerGate happened.

In August 2014, a harassment campaign organized on 4chan and Reddit targeted Zoë Quinn, the developer of *Depression Quest*. The stated justification was "ethics in games journalism." The actual mechanics were coordinated abuse — threats, doxxing, campaigns to destroy professional reputations. The campaign expanded to target Anita Sarkeesian and Brianna Wu. The "gamer identity" it claimed to defend was under threat, and the threat was this: the blank page had worked.

That's the thing about GamerGate that becomes clear in retrospect. The fury was not about journalism ethics. It was about the fact that *Depression Quest* existed and was being taken seriously. That a text game about what it feels like to be depressed — made in Twine, by a woman, distributed for free — was being reviewed in games publications alongside shooters and open-world RPGs. The blank page had produced work that the critical establishment was beginning to treat as important. That was the transgression.

You don't mobilize a harassment campaign against tools that aren't working.

---

Many of the games from the 2012–2014 Twine moment are already gone. *Consensual Torture Simulator* (2013): gone. *Conversations With My Mother* (2013): gone. A dozen others on the preservation lists: gone. They were HTML files hosted on personal sites, and the sites died. There's something almost fitting about that — this work was never meant to be institutional. It was personal. Made like poems, like sketches, like zines that circulate until they don't. Some of it was never meant to last.

But what it changed, lasts. By 2014, parser and parserless games were roughly equal in the IF Competition — a complete reversal from a few years earlier. The games press had acquired a language for autobiographical games, personal games, games that didn't need to win. Twine is still being used, itch.io is full of it, and somewhere right now someone is writing their first branching story in a free browser tool and finding out that the writer's vocabulary can be the game's vocabulary.

Porpentine wrote *Howling Dogs* in a week, during the first week of HRT, and it's still there. Still unbalancing people who stumble across it.

The stones wonder if it is interesting to suffer. I think it's interesting to persist.
